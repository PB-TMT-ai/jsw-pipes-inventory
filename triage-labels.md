# The P&T Operating System, in plain language

*One product, one distributor, one month — the whole flow with no jargon.*

**The cast:** Your company makes steel pipes at 2 plants. One product is a *40mm square tube* (used for gates and window grills). One distributor is *Sharma Steels* in Jaipur.

## Step 1 — Ask everyone what they'll need (around the 25th)

Your salesperson asks every distributor: "How much of each pipe will you need next month?" Sharma says: "100 tonnes of the 40mm square tube." Important: this is a **guess**, not a promise. He hasn't paid for anything yet.

## Step 2 — Clean up the guesses

From past months you know Sharma always asks for more than he buys — asks 100, buys 80, because asking high means he gets more if supplies run short. So you don't take his 100 at face value; you count it as roughly 80. Do this for every distributor and add it up. Now you have *your own* realistic expectation of next month — not the sum of everyone's exaggerations.

## Step 3 — Check what the factory can actually make

Add up demand for all pipes and compare with plant capacity. Say everyone wants 6,500 tonnes but plants can make 5,000. Someone gets less. The fair rule: split it based on what each distributor **actually bought in the last 3 months** — not what he claimed. Sharma, who claims 100 but buys 80, gets his fair 80. This kills the exaggeration habit, because exaggerating no longer earns anything.

## Step 4 — Make the factory timetable ("campaign")

The factory's core problem: switching the machine from 40mm to 50mm takes **hours of setup** and wastes material. So you never make every size every day. You batch: *Mon–Tue only 40mm. Wed–Thu only 50mm.* Each size gets its turn maybe once a fortnight.

Publish it like a train timetable:

> "40mm square tube is made on the 5th and the 19th. Want some? Order before the 3rd. Missed it? Next train is the 19th."

This solves two problems at once: the factory runs efficiently, and distributors learn to order on time instead of calling in panic.

## Step 5 — Make it, ship it

The factory follows the timetable. Trucks go out. Sharma gets his 80 tonnes.

## Step 6 — Sharma sells it onward (the blind spot)

Sharma sells to hardware shops and fabricators around Jaipur. **You can't see this part.** Your records show "sold 80 tonnes to Sharma" — but did *he* sell 80, or is it sitting in his godown? You only find out if he reports it. The trick every big company uses: tie his monthly discount to submitting a simple report — "what I sold, what's left." No report, no rebate. Now the data comes on its own.

## Step 7 — Watch the gaps ("deviation")

At every step above, something was *supposed* to happen. The system compares supposed-to vs actually-happened and raises a flag when the gap is big:

- Sharma guessed 100, ordered 60 → **flag.** Careless guessing, or his market dropped — someone should ask.
- Factory planned 500 tonnes on the 5th, made 350 because an "urgent" order barged in → **flag**, with the reason recorded, so you can see who keeps breaking the timetable.
- You sold Sharma 80, 80, 80 over three months but he sold onward 60, 55, 50 — his godown is filling → **flag.** Stop pushing stock; help him sell. This is how companies quietly get into trouble: sales look fine while the pipeline silently clogs.
- Sharma's unpaid bills cross his credit limit → **orders blocked automatically** until a named person reviews it.

One rule makes this work: **every flag has one named owner and one fixed meeting where it's discussed.** A flag nobody owns is decoration on a dashboard.

## Step 8 — Repeat, and get smarter

Next month the loop runs again — but now you know whose guesses to trust, which pipes sell steadily (keep in stock always), and which sell rarely (make only against firm orders).

---

That's the entire operating system. Not complicated software — this loop, written as rules, with the numbers in one place, and alarm bells when reality drifts from plan.
